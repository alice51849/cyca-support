Irregular periods: how to track them so the pattern finally makes sense

If your period shows up after 24 days one month and 40 the next, a calendar that only marks “day 28” is useless to you. Tracking still helps, but it has to be done differently: log the start date and a few signals every cycle, give it about three months, and read the range rather than a single predicted date. This page walks through that method, what US health agencies say counts as irregular, and what to take to your doctor.

What counts as irregular

The Office on Women’s Health (OWH) describes a typical cycle as 28 days but notes that many people differ; periods count as regular when they usually come every 24 to 38 days. OWH suggests talking to a doctor or nurse if your cycles are consistently shorter than 24 days or longer than 38, or if you go 90 days without a period while not pregnant or breastfeeding.

Some irregularity is expected at certain times. OWH says cycles longer than 38 days are common in the first few years after a first period, and that cycles often become irregular in your 40s during the transition to menopause. Outside those windows, a cycle that swings widely from month to month is worth logging carefully and discussing.

Polycystic ovary syndrome is one common reason. OWH lists irregular cycles as a main symptom of PCOS, including periods that come fewer than eight times a year or every 21 days or more often, and irregular periods are one of the criteria doctors use when diagnosing it. The NICHD describes PCOS as a set of symptoms tied to a hormonal imbalance, diagnosed by two or more features, one of which is absent ovulation leading to light or skipped periods.

The three-month method

With irregular cycles you are not trying to predict one date. You are trying to learn your range and the signals that come before a period. That needs a few cycles of data, so commit to three months before you judge anything.

  1. Log day 1 every time. OWH’s advice is simply to mark the first day of each period on a calendar so you can see whether your cycles are regular. Day 1 is the first day of real bleeding, not spotting.
  2. Log flow for every bleeding day. Light, medium, heavy. For irregular cycles this separates a true period from spotting between periods.
  3. Log two or three symptoms, not twenty. Pick the ones you notice most: cramps, breast tenderness, skin changes, mood, sleep. OWH mentions tracking PMS symptoms, how heavy the bleeding is, and period pain that affects school or work.
  4. Write the cycle length down when the next period starts. After three cycles you have three numbers, say 26, 41 and 33, and the honest statement is “my cycles run about 26 to 41 days,” not “my cycle is 33 days.”
  5. Note anything unusual the same day. Travel, illness, a new medication, a big stress week. These are the first things a clinician will ask about.

Keep the log in one place you will actually open daily. Paper works. A notes app works. A period app works if it lets you log in seconds and keeps the data where you want it.

What to bring to your appointment

A clinician can do far more with three months of dates than with “it’s kind of all over the place.” Bring:

  • The start date of each period for the last three to six cycles, and the cycle lengths you calculated.
  • How many bleeding days each time and roughly how heavy.
  • Any bleeding between periods. ACOG’s page on abnormal uterine bleeding covers bleeding between periods, after sex, heavier or longer than usual, and cycles outside the usual range.
  • The symptoms you logged and when in the cycle they clustered.
  • Medications, supplements, recent weight change, and whether you are trying to conceive.

If you are using an app, a screenshot of the calendar or the logged history is enough; you do not need to export anything or share the app itself.

If you are trying to conceive with irregular cycles

Calendar-based fertile window estimates assume a fairly stable gap between ovulation and the next period. When cycle length varies by two weeks, the estimated window can be wrong by the same amount. Frequently asked questions

How irregular is too irregular?

OWH suggests talking to a doctor or nurse if your cycles are consistently shorter than 24 days or longer than 38, or if you have gone 90 days without a period and are not pregnant or breastfeeding. Bleeding between periods or much heavier bleeding than usual is also worth a visit; see ACOG’s page on abnormal uterine bleeding.

Can an app predict my period if my cycles are irregular?

Only as an estimate, and a wider one than for regular cycles. Cyca’s listing says it learns your rhythm and adapts every time you log, so give it several cycles of data and read the prediction as a range. Cyca is not a medical device or a form of contraception.

Is tracking even worth it if I have PCOS?

Yes. OWH lists irregular periods as a diagnostic criterion for PCOS, so a dated record of when periods did and did not come is useful to the clinician. The log also shows whether a treatment is changing your pattern over time.
