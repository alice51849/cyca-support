Late period: common reasons, when to test, and when to see a doctor

When a period is late, pregnancy is usually the first thought. The NHS says late or missed periods can be caused by many things and most of the time it is nothing to worry about, but it is still worth seeing a doctor just in case. Below are the common causes, when a pregnancy test is reliable, and the signs that mean you should get checked. This is general information, not a way to diagnose yourself.

First: what counts as late?

According to the NHS, regular cycles from 21 to 35 days are normal and the average is around 28 days, and it is common for cycles to vary by a few days. So before calling a period late, compare it with your own pattern. If you usually have a 30-day cycle, day 31 without a period is not necessarily unusual. If you are normally like clockwork and you are more than a week late, it is worth paying attention.

This is where a cycle log pays off: knowing your last few cycle lengths is the only way to tell whether this one really is late.

Common reasons for a late or missed period

Based on the NHS page on missed or late periods. This is a general list; only a doctor can tell you what is behind yours.

Possible reasonWhat it means in general
PregnancyThe first thing to rule out if you have had sex and your period has not come.
StressWork, exams or emotional strain can affect hormones and delay ovulation.
Sudden weight lossLosing a lot of weight quickly can make periods stop.
Being overweightCan also affect how regular your cycle is.
Too much exerciseIntense training or a sudden jump in activity can make periods lighter or stop.
PCOSIrregular periods or long gaps are a common sign; the NHS now calls it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS).
Some hormonal contraceptionThe pill, the contraceptive injection or an IUS can change bleeding patterns.
BreastfeedingPeriods often have not returned, or are irregular, while breastfeeding.
PerimenopauseUsually between 45 and 55 according to the NHS; before 45 it may be early menopause and should be checked.
Other medical conditionsThe NHS lists heart disease, diabetes and an overactive or underactive thyroid among conditions that can stop periods.

The NHS also notes that for some people the gap between periods simply varies each time, which is known as irregular periods.

When a pregnancy test is reliable

From the NHS guidance on doing a pregnancy test:

  • Most tests can be used from the first day of a missed period, which is when they are most reliable.
  • If you do not know when your period is due, test at least 21 days after you last had unprotected sex.
  • You can use urine from any time of day; it does not have to be the first in the morning.
  • A positive result is almost certainly correct. A negative result is less reliable, for example if you tested too early or did not follow the instructions. If it is negative and you still think you might be pregnant, wait a few days and test again; if a second test is negative and your period still has not arrived, speak to your GP.

The NHS also points out that hormonal contraception does not stop a pregnancy test from working, and that no method of contraception is completely effective.

When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a GP (non-urgent) if:

  • you have missed your period 3 times in a row
  • your periods have not started by the time you are 15
  • you have missed your period and have other symptoms such as weight gain or weight loss, tiredness, hair growth on your face, or dry or oily skin
  • your periods have become irregular
  • you have had two negative pregnancy tests and your period still has not come
If you have severe tummy pain, very heavy bleeding, dizziness or fainting, or bleeding when you know you are pregnant, do not wait: get urgent medical help.

A doctor will usually ask about your cycle history and lifestyle, may arrange blood tests, and may refer you to a gynaecologist or endocrinologist. A clear period record makes that appointment much more useful.

What to bring to the appointment

  • Start dates and length of your last 3 to 6 periods.
  • Roughly how heavy each one was: heavy, medium or light.
  • Any recent stress, weight change, change in exercise, or medication started or stopped.
  • Other symptoms: tiredness, acne, hair changes, low mood, pain.
  • Dates and results of any pregnancy tests.

Frequently asked questions

How late is too late for a period?

It depends on your normal pattern. The NHS treats cycles of 21 to 35 days as normal and a few days’ variation is common. Comparing with your own history is more useful than a fixed number; missing 3 periods in a row is a reason to see a GP.

When should I take a pregnancy test?

The NHS says most tests work from the first day of a missed period. If you do not know when it is due, test at least 21 days after unprotected sex, and retest after a few days if it is negative but you still suspect pregnancy.

Can stress really delay a period?

Yes. The NHS lists stress among the common causes of late or missed periods. Pregnancy should still be ruled out first.

Does a late period mean I have PCOS?

Not necessarily. Irregular periods or long gaps are a common sign of PCOS (now called PMOS by the NHS), but there are many other causes. A doctor diagnoses it using your history, blood tests and sometimes an ultrasound.
