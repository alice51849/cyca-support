Tracking your cycle with PCOS: what to log and a template you can copy

One of the most common frustrations with PCOS is irregular periods with long gaps, which makes “when will it come?” almost impossible to guess. Instead of relying on a calculator, it often helps more to keep a careful record and hand your doctor clear information. This page is only about tracking. It does not cover treatment or outcomes; diagnosis and treatment belong with your doctor.

PCOS and your period (general information)

The World Health Organization describes PCOS as a common hormonal disorder affecting an estimated 10 to 13% of women of reproductive age, and says up to 70% of those affected worldwide do not know they have it. WHO calls it the most common cause of anovulation and lists irregular or infrequent periods, abnormal ovulation, hair changes, oily skin and acne among its signs.

The NHS now uses the name polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) for what used to be called PCOS. Its main symptoms list includes irregular periods or long gaps between them, excess hair growth or hair loss, weight gain or difficulty losing weight, difficulty getting pregnant, depression or anxiety, oily skin and acne, feeling very tired, and dark, thick patches of skin on the neck or armpits. The NHS also stresses that these symptoms are common to many conditions, so having them does not mean you definitely have it, but it is important to get them checked.

Plain-text version for a notes app:
Period #__ | Start: __/__ | End: __/__ | Days since last: __ | Flow: H/M/L | Symptoms: __ | Mood: __ | Notes: __

After 3 to 6 months, add a one-line summary at the top: shortest cycle, longest cycle, and whether there were any gaps of more than two or three months. That line is often the first thing a doctor wants to know.

How to bring your log to the doctor

  1. Start with a three-sentence summary, for example: “3 periods in the last 6 months, 45 to 80 days apart, fairly light, more acne than before.”
  2. Flag anything unusual: very long gaps, bleeding between periods, especially painful or heavy periods.
  3. Write down your questions: which tests might be needed, and is there anything else worth tracking?
  4. Bring a medication list, including supplements and contraception.

The NHS says that if a GP suspects the condition, they may arrange blood tests for hormone levels and insulin resistance, or an ultrasound of the ovaries. ACOG likewise describes evaluation by a doctor based on your situation. Follow your doctor on tests and treatment; this page gives no treatment information.

If you are trying to conceive, mention it, since both WHO and the NHS note that PCOS can affect ovulation and getting pregnant.

Frequently asked questions

Does everyone with PCOS have irregular periods?

No. The NHS says symptoms can be mild or severe, or absent altogether; irregular periods or long gaps are one common sign.

Are ovulation calculators accurate with PCOS?

Usually not very. Calendar methods depend on regular cycles, and WHO describes PCOS as the most common cause of anovulation. Ask your doctor whether other ovulation testing makes sense for you.

How long should I track before my appointment?

For premenstrual symptoms ACOG suggests recording for at least 2 to 3 months. Because PCOS cycles are often long, several months and several periods give a clearer picture; your doctor may ask for more.

Has PCOS been renamed?

The NHS now uses the name polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) and notes it used to be called PCOS. WHO and ACOG pages still use PCOS.
