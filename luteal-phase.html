What is the luteal phase? The four cycle phases and PMS, explained

The luteal phase is the stretch between ovulation and your next period. The NHS says the time from the release of an egg to the start of a period is around 10 to 16 days, and ACOG notes this is the most consistent part of the cycle. Below is a table of the four phases, how luteal length fits with ovulation timing, and what it has to do with PMS.

The four phases of the menstrual cycle

The cycle starts on the first day of your period. The timings below use a common 28-day cycle; your own will differ (the NHS treats 21 to 35 days as normal).

PhaseRough timing (28-day cycle)What is happening
MenstruationFrom day 1, about 2 to 7 daysThe womb lining is shed; the NHS says periods last around 2 to 7 days.
Follicular phaseDay 1 until ovulationOestrogen rises, follicles develop in the ovary and the womb lining thickens. This is the part whose length varies most.
OvulationAround day 14An ovary releases an egg; ACOG says the egg survives about 24 hours.
Luteal phaseFrom ovulation to the next period, about 10 to 16 daysProgesterone prepares the womb lining for implantation; if there is no pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone fall and a period starts.

Note that menstruation and the follicular phase overlap: the follicular phase starts on day 1 and runs until ovulation. Many apps and articles draw them separately simply because it is easier to follow.

How long is the luteal phase?

The NHS describes the time from egg release to the start of a period as around 10 to 16 days. ACOG says that in a 28-day cycle ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period, which is exactly why ovulation calculators count back 14 days.

Because the luteal phase is fairly steady, most differences in cycle length come from the follicular phase. For example:

  • 28-day cycle with a 14-day luteal phase: ovulation around day 14 to 15.
  • 35-day cycle with a 14-day luteal phase: ovulation around day 21 to 22.
  • 24-day cycle with a 12-day luteal phase: ovulation around day 12 to 13.

If you think your luteal phase may be unusually short or something else seems off, take your records to a doctor. A calendar estimate from an app cannot tell you whether your luteal phase is working normally.

The luteal phase and PMS

Premenstrual syndrome happens before your period, in the later part of the luteal phase. ACOG explains that many women notice physical or mood changes in the days before menstruation, and when these happen month after month and affect normal life, they are known as PMS.

Common symptoms listed by ACOG include:

  • Emotional: low mood, irritability, anxiety, crying spells, poor concentration, insomnia.
  • Physical: breast tenderness, bloating, headache, swelling of the hands or feet, fatigue, appetite changes, skin problems.

According to ACOG, a diagnosis looks for symptoms present in the 5 days before a period for at least three cycles in a row, ending within 4 days after the period starts, and interfering with normal activities. That is why ACOG suggests writing down and rating your symptoms each day, along with your period dates, for at least 2 to 3 months.

If symptoms are severe enough to cause problems at work or in relationships, it could be premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), or another condition such as depression, anxiety or thyroid disease that gets worse before a period. Please see a doctor rather than deciding on your own.

Frequently asked questions

Is the luteal phase always 14 days?

No. The NHS says the time from ovulation to a period is around 10 to 16 days; 14 is just the figure commonly used for a 28-day cycle.

What does the luteal phase feel like?

It varies. Some people notice nothing, others have premenstrual symptoms such as breast tenderness, bloating or mood changes in the days before a period (ACOG). If symptoms affect daily life, see a doctor.

Can you get pregnant during the luteal phase?

ACOG says pregnancy is possible from about 5 days before ovulation to 1 day after, so most of the luteal phase falls outside that window. But ovulation is hard to pinpoint, so never treat the luteal phase as a “safe” time for contraception.

How is PMS confirmed?

By a doctor. ACOG suggests recording symptoms and period dates every day for at least 2 to 3 months so a clear premenstrual pattern can be seen.
